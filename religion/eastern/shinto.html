Shintō

Shintō (Japanese: 神道), also called Shintoism, is a religion from Japan. In modern Japan it is primarily a general term for "traditional Japanese religious practices" and "Japan's indigenous religions". There is no single definition of Shinto in the modern sense, nor is there a single meaning of "Shinto" historically. It is sometimes classified as a nature religion. With no central authority, there is much diversity in belief and practices.

Shinto is polytheistic and animistic. It revolves around supernatural entities called the kami, who are believed to inhabit all things, including forces of nature and prominent landscape locations. The kami are worshipped (not only) at (among other things: household, family and public) shrines. People worship kami to cultivate harmony between humans and kami and to receive blessings. Shinto places a major focus on ensuring purity. largely by cleaning practices such as ritual washing and bathing, especially before worship. The dead are capable of becoming kami. The religion has no single creator or specific doctrine, and instead exists in a diverse range of local and regional forms.

According to Western sources, the first traces, although it is debatable whether they can be classified as religion, date back to Japan's Yayoi period (300 BCE to 250 CE). Japanese academia mainly believes that Shintō was established in the late 7th or 8th century during the reigns of Emperor Tenmu and Empress Jito under the Ritsuryo legal system, or at the latest by the 15th century when its doctrines were organized and systematized, and it became independent from Buddhism.

For readability reasons, the terms traditional Japanese religious practices, Japan's indigenous religions, Shintō believe, Kami belief and Shintō will be denoted with Shintō.
